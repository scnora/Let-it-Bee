Worker Bee

Worker bees are one of the most important bees in the hive. Contrary to popular belief, worker bees are actually female bees. They are responsible for maintaining the hive, gathering nectar, and caring for the Larvae. These roles are dependent on the age of the worker bee. When they are young, they are nurse bees, which means they are responsible for taking care of the young Larvae. They are also in charge of processing the incoming nectar and feeding the queen. Once they start getting a bit over, they are in charge of capping the honeycombs with wax they produce.

When they are older, they are in charge of leaving the Hive and collecting resources for the colony to survive. They are responsible for gathering nectar, pollen, water, and propolis. Almost all the bees you seeing flying around are worker bees and they make up most of the hive population.

Drone Bee

image by Sue Boo from USGS Bee Lab

Drone bees are the only male bees in the hive. They are primarily responsible for mating with the Queen bee. They are slightly larger than a Worker bee and have bigger eyes in order to find the queen. They do not have stingers and don't gather nectar or pollen.

Drone bees only live during spring and summer and they die instantly die after mating. These bees are interesting in that they consume a lot of resources but cannot feed themselves. Funnily enough, worker bees often evict drones from the hives in autumn in order to conserve food for winter.

Queen Bee

Image by Jonathan Wilkins from Wikimedia Commons

Queen bees are the only reproductive female in the hive and are the main bee in a hive. They are essential for a colony surivial and she is the largest bee in the hive. They are usually 15-20 mm long and have a long tapered abdomen and a specialized unbarbed stinger. Queen bees are developed in large queen cells and are fed royal jelly, which speeds up her maturity. This royal jelly is fed to all bees for at least 3 days, and the queen is the only bee to continue to feast on it throughout her development.

The Queen Bee is responsible for laying about 2,000 eggs per day and they need to produce phermones that stabilize the colony. She is the mother of all the bees in the hive and her genetics effect the colony and its size. A strong and healthy queen is a good sign of having a healthy colony.

Larvae

Image by Waugsberg from Wikimedia Commons

Larvae are the young bees that are still developing in the hive. They are completely dependent on worker bees for survival during this stage. They are fed a diet of royal jelly at first, and then pollen and honey as they grow. Over time, they increase in size rapidly and begin to fill the cell they are in. Once fully grown, the cell is sealed with wax and the larvae begins its transformation into an adult bee.

The larvae stage is one of the most important parts of a bee’s life cycle. During this time, they grow quickly and prepare for their transformation. After the cell is sealed, the larvae spins a cocoon and begins to change into a pupa. This process eventually leads to the development of a fully formed adult bee ready to emerge.

Queen Markings

Image by Steve Burt from Flickr

Queen markings are small colored dots that beekeepers place on the queen bee. These markings make it easier to quickly identify the queen among thousands of bees in the hive. Each color represent the year the queen was born, helping beekeepers track her age. This is important because a younger queen usually means a stronger and more productive colony.

An easy way to remember the colors for each year is by this fun mnemonic device: "Will you Raise Good Bees".

The starting letter of each word represents a color (White, Yellow, Red, Green, and Blue). White are for years that end in 1 or 6 (e.g. 2021, 2026, 2031,etc.) Yellow are for years ending in 2 or 7. Red is for years ending in 3 or 8. Green are for years ending in 4 or 9. Lastly, Blue are for years ending in 5 or 0. Since we are in 2026, You will see your youngest local Queen Bees marked white!

Honeycombs

Honeycombs are the hexagonal cell structure made of beeswax. These cells are made by honeybees and are used to store honey, pollen, and Larvae. This structure is effective in store max amount of honey with minimum wax production. These wax structures are edible and filled with many nurtrients.

The hexagonal shape of the honeycomb is not random and is extremely efficient. This design makes the structure strong enough to hold the weight of honey and developing bees. Because of this, honeycombs are one of the most recognizable and important parts of the hive.

Honey

Honey is created when worker bees collect nectar from flowers and bring it back to the hive. Inside the hive, the nectar is broken down and stored in the honeycombs. The bees then fan their wings to remove moisture, turning the nectar into thick honey. It serves as the main food source for the colony, especially during colder months.

Honey is not only important for bees, but it is also widely used by humans. It has natural antibacterial properties and can be used in food, medicine, and skincare. Different types of flowers produce different flavors and colors of honey. This is why honey can vary in taste depending on where it is made.

Wax

Beeswax is produced by worker bees from special glands on their bodies. They use this wax to build and repair the honeycomb structure inside the hive. The wax starts as small flakes that the bees chew and shape into hexagonal cells. These cells are used to store honey, pollen, and to raise new larvae.

Beeswax is also useful outside of the hive and has many human uses. It is commonly used in candles, cosmetics, and food products. The wax is natural and biodegradable, making it an environmentally friendly material. Because of its versatility, beeswax has been valued by humans for thousands of years.
